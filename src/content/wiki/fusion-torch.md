Ships in 2325 are pushed by **torches**: fusion drives burning [helium-3](/wiki/helium-3/) and deuterium. A torch turns the solar system from years of travel into weeks. It also makes flying a matter of weight, heat and money.

## Burns and coasts

- A **burn** is firing the main drive. To **light** the torch, or **light for** a place, is to start one.
- A **coast** is flying with the drive off. Everyone floats until the next burn.
- Acceleration is felt as weight. A gentle cruise is a fraction of a gee. An emergency burn can be one, two, three gees or more, and crews ride it in [crash couches](/wiki/zero-g-medicine/).
- There are no brakes. To slow down, a ship **flips** end over end and burns the other way.
- Small thrusters all over the hull, the **RCS**, turn the ship and nudge it. Moving a ship sideways or forward on thrusters without turning it is **translation**.

**Delta-v** is the budget of speed changes a ship has left in its tanks. Run out of reaction mass and you are going wherever you are pointed. That is called going **ballistic**.

## The drive

The engine’s business end is the **nozzle**, or **bell**: the cone the plasma leaves through. Bells wear. Metal erodes on long burns, and engineers watch for the **wear plateau**, when erosion levels off, and the **structural flag**, when a sensor says it is time to replace it.

## The stamp

Inside USN jurisdiction, parts have to carry **Sovereign Vendor Certification**. A certified drive bell through the USN depot costs about **42,000 shu**. A Belt-built bell of the same quality runs about **14,000**, but without the stamp a ship can’t work USN contracts. *Ship stays compliant or ship stays docked.* Many Capa captains fly on old certified parts as long as the numbers let them.

## Getting there

Even with fusion, the cheapest path between planets is a long, slow **Hohmann transfer**. Freight that can wait takes it. A torch lets a ship trade fuel for time, and in an emergency, time is the thing you buy.


<aside class="science">

## Behind the science

Fusion rockets are a serious engineering idea. One design, the Direct Fusion Drive developed from Princeton’s field-reversed-configuration experiments, is specifically meant to burn deuterium and helium-3. Its charged exhaust can be steered by magnetic nozzles.

Flip-and-burn is simply how you stop in a vacuum. Burns measured in gees are how pilots and astronauts already describe acceleration. The book’s one big assumption is that clean fusion became reliable enough to bolt onto a ship.
