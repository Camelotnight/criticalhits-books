A **backpacker** is a hard-shell spacesuit built for long, heavy work outside: survey, salvage, construction and extended rescue. It is named for the big life-support pack on its back that gives it its endurance.

## What it is

Standard EVA suits are for quick orbital maintenance. A backpacker is the long-haul kind:

- **Thick composite** shell over folded radiators.
- **Heavy packs of monopropellant** for maneuvering. They burn hot and bright and bloom in infrared with every touch of the throttle.
- **Independent life support** for hours of work.
- An **industrial chassis**, strong enough to pry open crushed bulkheads.

Smart backpackers can change how their skin radiates heat: dark and glowing to shed heat when the wearer runs hot, shiny to hold it in when they are cold. That stretches the suit’s endurance a long way.

## A suit inside a suit

Capa crews wear a backpacker over a [Slick suit](/wiki/slick-suit/). If the hard shell is lost or has to be abandoned, the Slick underneath still holds the body together. Capa engineers are paranoid about redundancy, and people who have climbed out of a dead backpacker are grateful for it. In the chest bay rides a **Last Breath**: a transparent emergency pressure hood folded to the size of a datapad.

## Backpacker or slick?

| | Backpacker | Slick suit |
|---|---|---|
| Built for | Long EVA, heavy work | Short EVA, work and breach |
| Shell | Rigid composite | Elastic weave |
| Endurance | Long | Short |
| Fits through | Open spaces | Almost anything |

The trade is real. A hard shell has a fixed cross-section. Force it through a warped bulkhead gap and it wedges.

*Hercules construction site, L4. Item: long-endurance hard-shell suit, Belt pattern, worn over a Slick suit. Drill supervised by the Capa crew chief.*

**Question.** The backpacker is a complete pressure suit worn over a second complete pressure suit. By our design rules the inner garment is redundant mass. I was asked whether it could be deleted.

**Method.** One full work shift on the lattice in an issued shell, followed by the crew’s standard shell-loss drill. The chief declares a shell dead. The wearer fits the emergency hood from the chest bay, opens the shell and leaves it. The hood is called the Last Breath. I asked whether the name was official. The chief said it was accurate.

**Observation.** Through the shift the shell managed its own heat. The panels darkened under load to shed heat and brightened in shadow to hold it. No input was asked of me. On the drill I left a dead shell in vacuum wearing the Slick and the hood, with both hands free and no loss of pressure on the body. The crew’s apprentices ran the same drill at the end of the same shift. They were faster. They were also carrying tools.

**Finding.** The shell supplies endurance, thrust and strength. The Slick keeps the wearer alive when the shell stops. Crews treat the shell as equipment and the Slick as clothing.

**Assessment.** Recommend against deleting the inner garment. Our form lists redundancy as a deficiency. I have entered it under safety features and attached this note.

**Note.** A hard shell has one cross-section and does not negotiate. Gaps are measured before entry. The crew chief measures them himself.

## Behind the science

Hard suits are real and old. NASA’s spacewalking suit uses a rigid upper torso, and hard-shell suits have been built for deep-sea and space work for decades.

The book’s twist is the skin. **Variable-emittance** coatings, such as vanadium dioxide, switch how strongly they radiate infrared as their temperature changes. A suit that can choose when to glow and when to hold its heat needs far less water for cooling, and can stay out much longer.
