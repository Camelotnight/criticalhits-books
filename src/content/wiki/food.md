## Why everything is spicy

In low weight, the body’s fluids drift toward the head. The sinuses fill and the tongue stops reporting much beyond salt and heat. Station cooks answer with chili, garlic, fermented pastes and salt, and most Capa carry their own bottle of hot sauce.

## How it is grown

- **Fermentation.** Yeast makes milk proteins, so skyr exists without cows.
- **Insects.** Crickets turn plant stalks and scraps into protein on a fraction of the water fish need.
- **Algae.** Spirulina and chlorella in clear tubes scrub carbon dioxide and are milled into flour.
- **Aeroponics.** Sweet potatoes hang with bare roots in a nutrient mist, saving water and mass.
- **Cultured meat.** Real animal cells grown in bioreactors. The serums and power make it expensive, so it stays a payday or upper-tier food.
- **Fungi.** Mushroom protein grows in the warm, humid decks of the Sump.

## Ganymede’s table

Ganymede has more water than it can use, and Rodwells, warm reservoirs melted into the ice, where the fishery began. Its surplus is shared with Callisto. Its signature is salt and the meaty taste of mushrooms. Mussels, mushroom meat and vat-cultured skyr are everyday food, and a plain whey drink stands in for water. The company spirit is cheap and very strong.

The ice carries bitter magnesium salts, so local water and local salt have a taste newcomers dislike and lifers miss. There is no open flame anywhere, because the air is thin and oxygen-rich. Nothing is charred or smoked, water boils cooler, tea comes out weak, and cooking is done under pressure. With vacuum and cold just outside the wall, freeze-drying costs nothing.

*Minerva Station, transit concourse, L4. Item: station food, Capa pattern, from the kiosk menu. Reference item: USN field ration, certified. Tasting led by my Capa assistant, a hull tech assigned to me for the trials.*

**Method.** The standard scores a ration by calories, protein and shelf life. It does not score taste. I ate the reference ration first, then worked down the kiosk menu over three shifts.

**Observation.** The reference ration meets every line of the standard. On the station it tastes of nothing. This happens to everyone up here. In low weight the body’s fluid drifts toward the head, the sinuses fill, and the tongue reports only salt and heat. The Capa food is built for that tongue. The jerky comes in a chili-garlic glaze. The congee carries ginger and fish sauce. The skyr is the one cold, tart thing on the menu, and I ordered it twice.

**Field practice.** My assistant carries a squeeze bottle of hydroponic chili sauce on a lanyard. He added it to the congee, to the jerky, which was already glazed, to the sweet potato wedges, and on the second day to the skyr. Asked about the skyr, he said it was too quiet. Every Capa at the counter carried a bottle. No two bottles were the same.

**Finding.** The kiosk food beats the reference on calories per liter of water used, and on morale by any measure I could apply. At the end of the trial the reference ration was left on the counter. Nobody took it.

**Assessment.** Nonconforming. The standard does not measure flavor. I recommend that it should, and that the field ration ship with a bottle.

## Behind the science

The taste problem is real. Since the 1960s astronauts have reported that food tastes bland in orbit, and hot sauce is a fixture on the International Space Station. One leading explanation is fluid shifting toward the head, which stuffs the nose like a head cold; smell makes up much of what we call taste. Precision fermentation, insect protein, spirulina, aeroponics and cultured meat are all real technologies today. Their costs are what sort them into everyday food and payday food.
