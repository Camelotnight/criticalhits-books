Hulls in this world are [carbon weave](/wiki/carbon-weave/), and magnets don’t stick to carbon. So nobody wears magnetic boots. Spacers walk on **gecko grip**.

## How it works

The soles of **gecko boots** are covered in billions of microscopic stalks called **setae**. Each splits into still finer tips that touch a surface so closely that the molecules themselves attract. The pull is tiny per hair, enormous in total. A pad the size of a palm can hold more than a hundred kilograms.

The grip is **directional**. Press and pull back and it holds. Peel the toe up and it lets go. Walking in gecko boots is close to walking normally: catch, release, catch, release. There is none of the stomp-and-toggle of magnets.

## Words

- **Gecko boots**: the footwear.
- **Gecko pads**: everything else that grips, like knees, gloves, sleds and gear.
- **Gecko grip**: a deck surface built for pads to hold on.

## Failure

Gecko grip has one enemy: **fouling**. Dust, regolith, oil or blood clogs the hairs and kills the hold. Pads shed dust by vibrating ultrasonically. As a backup, many carry **electroadhesion**: a high-voltage field that clamps the pad to almost any surface, with a faint hum in the suit audio.

Setae also wear. An old boot that has walked a hundred hulls is worn smooth in places, and it takes a half-beat longer to catch than its partner.


<aside class="science">

## Behind the science

Real geckos climb glass using van der Waals forces through millions of branching hairs on their toes, and release by peeling. Engineers have copied this. Stanford researchers built gecko-inspired adhesives and climbing robots. NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory has tested gecko grippers for grabbing objects in microgravity, including on the International Space Station.

The rule that magnets are useless on hulls is the physics the book teaches hardest. It falls straight out of a world built from carbon.

</aside>
