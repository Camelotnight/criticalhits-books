People expect space to be cold. The real problem is the opposite. In a vacuum there is no air or water to carry heat away. A ship, a station or a suit can only lose heat by **radiating** it as infrared light, slowly, from its surface. Every machine, every reactor and every warm body adds heat, and all of it has to go somewhere.

## The Curtain

A radiator is called a **curtain**, and a ship’s radiator array is **the Curtain**. The best kind is a **liquid droplet radiator**. It sprays a sheet of hot droplets across open space, lets them glow off their heat, and catches them cold on the far side. The droplets look like a falling curtain. A droplet sheet can be huge and still weigh very little.

Solid radiators made of graphene fins do the same job where droplets won’t work. Ships keep their curtains in their own shadow, away from the Sun and from debris. On the *Cooper’s Reach* they deploy in the aft shadow of the hull.

When a curtain fails, the place it serves starts to cook. A station drum with a cracked radiator holds its heat; the air thickens and the recyclers pound. A dying ship whose reactor is still running heats itself from the inside until something gives.

## Heat sets the population

A habitat can only hold as many people as its radiators can cool. Food is the surprise. Growing a person’s food under lights adds several kilowatts of waste heat per resident, often more than the homes and industry put together. On small, crowded worlds like Phobos, heat is rationed.

## Suits

A body at work makes three to five hundred watts of heat. Suits carry **PCM liners**: phase-change material, a kind of wax that melts as it soaks up heat. When the wax is all melted, the suit can’t cool you any more. Every spacewalk runs on a thermal clock. See [Slick suit](/wiki/slick-suit/).

## Behind the science

Radiating heat follows the Stefan–Boltzmann law: power equals emissivity, times a constant, times area, times temperature to the fourth power. A good radiator at room temperature (about 300 kelvin) sheds roughly 400 watts per square meter into deep space, less if the Sun is on it.

That is why radiators get so large, and why hotter radiators are prized: double the temperature and you shed sixteen times the heat. Liquid droplet radiators were studied seriously by NASA in the 1980s for exactly this reason. They promise enormous radiating area for very little mass.

The International Space Station’s radiators are among the biggest pieces of the whole structure, and they are there for the same reason.
