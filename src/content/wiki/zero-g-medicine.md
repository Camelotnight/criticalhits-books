Medicine out here has to deal with physics a surgeon on Earth never thinks about.

## Blood without gravity

In free fall, blood doesn’t run or drip. Surface tension takes over. A slow bleed forms a quivering **dome** over the wound that clings and grows. A fast one sprays and then breaks into floating globes. Sweat pools across a face instead of running off it. Vomit inside a helmet can drown you. That is why the *Cooper’s Reach* keeps its medbay on the [spin ring](/wiki/spin-gravity/): blood that drains, IV lines that run, tools that stay put.

## Acceleration

Hard burns are measured in gees. At three gees a body weighs three times its normal weight. Eyelids drag shut, vision narrows, and every breath fights the straps. Tiny blood vessels in the eyes can burst. Crews ride burns in **crash couches** that tilt them flat against the push and hold them in gel. The worse the burn, the flatter you lie.

Ships carry **cerebral flow support**: injectors primed to push medicine that keeps blood moving to the brain during a hard burn, so the crew doesn’t black out.

## Pressors

**Pressors** (vasopressors) are the drugs a medic gives before a long, dangerous job. They hold blood pressure up against a suit’s compression, sharpen the mind, and burn off the fog of a hard burn. They work for hours. When they wear off, they wear off hard: tremor, a blood-pressure crash, the works.

## Injuries of the trade

- **Crush syndrome.** After a crushing injury, damaged muscle floods the blood with poisons that can shut down the kidneys. Dialysis cleans the blood while the body recovers.
- **Smart braces.** They splint an injury, log vitals and fire a nerve block into the site.
- **Hemostatic foam** to seal wounds, and mechanical piston CPR, because chest compressions don’t work when the rescuer floats away with every push.


<aside class="science">

## Behind the science

Blood behaving like this has been observed in microgravity. Without gravity, surface tension dominates fluids at small scales, which engineers measure with the Bond number. Bleeding in space is a real concern in planning for long missions.

“Pressors” is real emergency and ICU shorthand for vasopressors. The book uses it instead of an invented “stim” on purpose. The g-force effects are the ones pilots and astronauts train for: grey-out, tunnel vision, burst capillaries, and fighting to breathe under load.

</aside>
