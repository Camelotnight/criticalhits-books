**Weave** is the everyday word for the material their world is made of: hulls, bulkheads, station frames, suits and tools. **Carbon weave**, **composite weave** and **weave composite** all mean the same thing. Engineers call it carbon-nanotube composite. Nobody else does.

## Why carbon

A carbon nanotube is a sheet of carbon atoms, one atom thick, rolled into a seamless tube. Bundled and woven, the tubes make a material many times stronger than steel at a fraction of the weight.

Weight is everything in space. Every kilogram has to be pushed, and a spinning habitat has to hold up its own mass against the spin. Steel can’t do it. A steel cylinder spun for gravity would tear itself apart at a radius of a few kilometers. Weave can build [cylinders hundreds of kilometers across](/wiki/hercules/).

## A world without iron

Because almost nothing is ferrous, **magnets are useless on hulls**. There are no magnetic boots. Crews walk on [gecko grip](/wiki/gecko-grip/) instead. Magnets survive where they make sense: door mag-locks, docking clamps, a sled brake, the occasional old station deck. Real iron hulls belong to antique inner-system freighters and scrap.

## What it feels like

- **Sound.** Woven carbon doesn’t ring or rattle; it absorbs. An old titanium habitat groans at every joint. A weave station is eerily quiet, and the hum of machinery comes up through the floor as a feeling more than a sound.
- **Age.** Weave darkens with heat. On an old ship, the fiber bundles near the engine are carbonized along the seams where years of drive heat cooked the resin.
- **Dust.** Construction sites are full of weave dust, fine and abrasive. It clogs clips and pits visors. When it comes back aboard on a suit and meets air in a repressurizing airlock, it oxidizes with a sharp, sulfurous smell, like spent gunpowder.

## Its cousins

**Graphene**, the same carbon as a flat sheet, is the heat manager. It is the material of radiators and sensors. See [Heat](/wiki/heat/).

## Behind the science

The strength gap is real. High-strength steel can hold about 1 to 2 gigapascals of tension. A single carbon nanotube has been measured at tens of gigapascals, and the theoretical limit is higher still. The hard part, then and now, is turning microscopic tubes into long, flawless fiber. That is the job of the [Arachne weavers](/wiki/arachne-weavers/).

Engineers compare materials by “breaking length”: how long a cable can hang before it snaps under its own weight. For steel it is tens of kilometers. For ideal nanotube fiber it is thousands. That ratio is the whole reason the habitats in this book are woven rather than welded.

</aside>
