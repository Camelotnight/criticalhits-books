Everyone worries about oxygen. The Capa worry about **nitrogen**.

Earth’s air is 78 percent nitrogen. It is the inert buffer that lets people breathe oxygen without fire risk and without oxygen poisoning. Plants need it to make protein. Habitats need it to keep pressure. And unlike water or carbon, the outer system has very little of it within easy reach.

## Where it is (and isn’t)

- **Rocky asteroids**: almost none.
- **Dark Trojan asteroids**: a little, locked in organic tar, a tenth of a percent up to around one percent by weight in the richest rock.
- **Jupiter**: plenty, as ammonia in its clouds. But Jupiter’s gravity is so deep that lifting it out costs more than it is worth.
- **Titan**: an atmosphere that is almost pure nitrogen, on a moon with weak gravity. The cheapest nitrogen in the solar system.

So the air inside the L4 stations comes from Saturn. [Titan Technate](/wiki/titan-technate/) tankers run nitrogen in to the **Hektor depot** among the Trojans, and from there it reaches [Minerva Station](/wiki/minerva-station/) and the construction at [Hercules](/wiki/hercules/). A cylinder full of air is a lot of nitrogen, and every bit of it is somebody else’s.

## Keeping it

Because it is so precious, every habitat guards its nitrogen. Leaks are hunted, and stations hold strategic reserves.


<aside class="science">

## Behind the science

Nitrogen in Trojan-type asteroids is real but scarce. Meteorites thought to come from the outer solar system, like the Tagish Lake meteorite that fell in Canada in 2000, carry nitrogen in their organic matter. It is isotopically “heavy,” a fingerprint of the cold outer disk where those bodies formed.

Titan really is a nitrogen world: about 98 percent nitrogen at roughly one and a half times Earth’s surface pressure, with surface gravity about a seventh of Earth’s. With fusion drives, distance mostly costs time while gravity costs fuel. Shipping from Saturn beats lifting out of Jupiter.

</aside>
