An **Arachne-class weaver** is a machine that makes [carbon-nanotube fiber](/wiki/carbon-weave/) continuously, at industrial scale. A factory full of them is a **spinnery**. The biggest is in the Sump of [Minerva Station](/wiki/minerva-station/), built for one purpose: spinning the filament for [Hercules](/wiki/hercules/).

## How it works

Carbon-bearing gas pours into a hot reaction chamber with a mist of catalyst particles floating in it. On each particle a nanotube begins to grow. The tubes tangle together into a fine black web, an aerogel “sock,” which is drawn off and twisted into fiber.

The Arachne’s trick is that its reaction chamber **spins**. Centrifuge arms at tens of thousands of RPM turn the gas into a smooth vortex. In a smooth flow the tubes grow long and parallel instead of short and knotted. Parallel tubes grip each other better, and the fiber is stronger.

## The noise

A spinnery is loud: a 110-decibel thrum and a subsonic throb you feel through the floor before you hear it. Workers on the transit benches above the extruders feel it in their chests.

## The clean floor

The weaving floor is sealed behind curtains of flowing air and kept sterile. One speck of dust, a micron across, caught in the fiber is a flaw. Under load, a flaw is where the fiber breaks.

## Feedstock

The carbon comes from Venus. Floating refineries in its clouds crack carbon dioxide into carbon and oxygen and ship the carbon out as slurry. Crates in the Sump are stenciled *Venusian Sourced — USN Atmospheric Division*. Sulfur from Venus’s acid clouds goes into the mix too, as a promoter that helps the catalyst grow tubes. See [The inner system](/wiki/inner-system/).


<aside class="science">

## Behind the science

The Arachne is built on a real process: **floating-catalyst chemical vapor deposition**. Researchers at the University of Cambridge in the early 2000s showed you could pull nanotube fiber straight out of a furnace, where the tubes form a sock-like aerogel that can be wound continuously.

Sulfur really is used as a promoter. A trace of a sulfur compound in the feed gas helps the iron catalyst grow long tubes. The real limit on the fiber’s strength is how well the tubes line up and how few defects they carry. That is why the book’s weavers spin, and why their floors are cleaner than an operating room.

</aside>
