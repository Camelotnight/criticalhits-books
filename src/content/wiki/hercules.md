**Hercules** is the name for the giant habitat cylinders the USN is building in the L4 [Trojans](/wiki/the-trojans/), about 50,000 kilometers from [Minerva Station](/wiki/minerva-station/). It is the largest construction project in human history.

## What it looks like

The finished cylinder turns slowly against the stars. Its hull is plated in **regolith cladding**: rock mined from the Trojan swarm, crushed and fused into an outer skin. The whole structure wears the grey-brown surface of a dozen processed asteroids. It is ancient-looking material doing new work. The cladding is armor against debris and radiation, and it is cheap, because the rock is already there. Docking traffic cycles at the endcap under strobing collar lights.

Its twin, still under construction, shows what the first one looked like before the cladding went on: bare [composite weave](/wiki/carbon-weave/) open to vacuum, with construction drones tracing patient arcs across the unfinished lattice.

## How it is built

- **The frame** is carbon-nanotube filament spun at Minerva’s [Arachne](/wiki/arachne-weavers/) spinnery from carbon shipped up out of Venus’s clouds.
- **The skin** is Trojan rock. Capa crews spent months on **nudge burns** moving whole asteroids into position for the cladding runs.
- **The labor** is drones and people. Logistics and assembly drones work every quadrant. Capa crews do the work drones can’t, for a [hazard premium](/wiki/glossary/#hazard-premium) on top of the shift rate.
- **The safety net** is a rescue cutter on contract. The nearest USN response to the construction envelope is six hours out, so it is cheaper to pay a Capa ship to stand by. See [Search and rescue](/wiki/search-and-rescue/).

## Getting in

Hercules has a **waiting list**, and USN settlers clear first. Capa crews who built it wait behind people who never touched a rock. Some are offered a guaranteed berth when their contract clears, if it doesn’t fill first. Word is there are real animals inside. Rabbits, even, if you can get on that waiting list too.

On a cylinder this size, nobody says “deck.” The ground is a concourse or a floor where people work, and a terrace or a garden where people with money live.


<aside class="science">

## Behind the science

Why build a cylinder instead of digging into an asteroid? Because spin gravity needs radius. A rotating cylinder hundreds of kilometers across can give a full, comfortable gee with a spin too slow for anyone to feel. Its inside surface can hold landscapes, farms and air.

The catch is the material. A spinning cylinder is trying to burst all the time, a load engineers call hoop stress. Only something like carbon nanotubes is strong enough for its weight to hold together at that size. Rock makes good armor but terrible structure. So Hercules is carbon on the inside and rock on the outside.

</aside>
