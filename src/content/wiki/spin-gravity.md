There is no artificial gravity in this universe. There is only **spin gravity**: weight made by turning. Spin a ring or a cylinder and everything inside is flung outward against the floor. That push feels like weight.

The unit is the **gee**. One gee is a body’s weight on Earth. Six-tenths of a gee is a bit more than half that.

## Small ring, fast spin

A ship can only fit a small ring, and a small ring has to spin fast. The *Cooper’s Reach* carries a drum sixteen meters across. To make six-tenths of a gee, it turns about 8.3 times a minute, once every 7.2 seconds. The crew are used to it. Visitors get spin-sick for a day or two. Turn your head too fast and the inner ear swears you are tumbling.

The ring exists for the medbay. A surgeon needs a floor. Blood has to drain and pool instead of rising in domes, IV lines need gravity to run, and tools need to stay where they are put. The galley and the crew bunks share the ring. The command deck stays outside it, unspun.

For hard burns, the drum **collapses and locks**. Thrust gravity, the push of the engine, is a different weight in a different direction, and the two never mix.

## Big cylinder, slow spin

A city-sized cylinder like [Minerva](/wiki/minerva-station/) is a different animal. It turns so slowly that nobody can feel it. Its weight changes only with distance from the axis, so the station is organized in **tiers** kilometers apart, from weightless at the core to 1.2 gee at the skin. Going *down* means going outward.

## Spin on a moon

On [Callisto](/wiki/callisto/), Skuld City’s ring spins inside a crater on a moon that has gravity of its own. The two pulls add, so true “down” sits nine degrees off the floor. People there walk **the lean**, and it takes three or four days to learn.

## Weight and the body

Bodies follow their gravity. Capa raised on low spin grow tall and light. People born in Minerva’s 1.2-gee Sump grow thick and strong. People from Earth move stiff-legged through light gravity, as if negotiating with it.


<aside class="science">

## Behind the science

The math is short: the felt weight equals the square of the spin rate times the radius. At eight meters of radius you need about 0.87 radians a second, which is 8.3 RPM, to make six-tenths of a gee. Do the numbers and they hold.

The comfort limit is the hard part. Studies of people in rotating rooms suggest most untrained people tolerate only a couple of RPM without nausea, and adapted people can push higher. That is why the *Reach*’s crew is fine and its guests are not. It is also why the big habitats are big: at hundreds of kilometers of radius, a full gee needs only a few turns an hour.

</aside>
