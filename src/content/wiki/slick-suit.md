A **Slick suit**, or just a **slick**, is the spacesuit Capa crews work in. It isn’t a balloon of air. It is a skin.

## How it works

An old-style spacesuit keeps you alive by filling with air, and that makes it stiff, like a balloon you have to bend from inside. A Slick works by **mechanical counter-pressure**. The fabric itself squeezes the body evenly, hard enough to stand in for air pressure on the skin. Only the head has air around it, in a sealed helmet.

That changes everything:

- **It moves.** A Slick gives nearly a naked body’s range of motion. You can climb, crawl, twist sideways through a gap.
- **It fails small.** A puncture in a balloon suit is a blowout. A cut in a Slick is a bruise, because the fabric around the hole keeps squeezing. A sheared bolt can gouge down a thigh and the weave stretches tight and holds.
- **It goes on fast.** The fabric is threaded with shape-memory alloy. Loose when it is off, it shrinks onto the body like a second skin at the touch of a switch.

## Layers

- **The pressure layer**: the elastic weave against the skin, with stiff lines sewn in so it doesn’t bunch.
- **PCM liners**: phase-change wax that soaks up body heat. When the wax is all melted, the suit can no longer cool you. See [Heat](/wiki/heat/).
- **The Thermal Micrometeoroid Garment**: a tougher outer shell over the top. Crews sometimes strip it off to fit through tighter spaces.
- **The helmet**: faceplates rated against micrometeoroids, with tear-off film layers to peel away smoke and soot.

For hot work, a crew can strap a **firefighting rig** over the suit: hydrogel cooling panels that buy about forty minutes before they liquefy.

## What it isn’t

A Slick is a short-trip suit for work and breaching. It gives **no ballistic protection**. For a long walk outside, a crew climbs into a [backpacker](/wiki/backpacker/), with the Slick still on underneath.

## Field notes

### Puncture response

<p class="byline">Dr. Gavin Macaraeg · USN materials researcher, Sovereign Vendor Certification</p>

*Achilles depot, L4. Item: counter-pressure work garment, Belt pattern. Test conducted by a senior Capa suit technician. The evaluator served as test subject.*

**Method.** The standard calls for a leak-rate test of the pressure bladder. The garment has no bladder. I asked the technician how her yard accepts a new suit. She said every Slick is holed once, on its wearer, at full vacuum, before the wearer goes on a work roster. A crew member should meet a puncture for the first time in a chamber, with a patch in reach. We followed the yard procedure.

**Observation.** Chamber at hard vacuum. The technician passed a steel awl through the left forearm sleeve. Helmet pressure held. The weave around the puncture kept its tension. The exposed skin cooled and lost sensation. The technician sealed the site with a thumb patch and logged the time.

**Finding.** One contusion, the width of the awl, resolved without treatment. In a gas-pressurized suit the same puncture is an emergency. In this garment it is a maintenance item.

**Limits.** Two were confirmed. The wax liners set a fixed thermal working time, and the weave gives no protection against a projectile. The crews plan around both.

**Assessment.** Nonconforming. The garment cannot pass a bladder test because it has no bladder to test. I have recorded the result as a failure of the form.

**Note.** The technician asked whether the USN procedure also tests the wearer. I said it did not. “Then you only know half of it,” she said. I have no technical objection.

<aside class="science">

## Behind the science

Mechanical counter-pressure suits are real research. Paul Webb built and tested a “Space Activity Suit” in the late 1960s. Dava Newman’s team at MIT has spent years on the BioSuit, a skin-tight design that uses tension along carefully mapped lines to press on the body. Shape-memory alloy coils that tighten the suit once it is on came out of that work too.

The hardest problems are getting even pressure into the hollows of the body, and getting the suit on and off. That is why the book’s Slick shrinks on at a switch.
